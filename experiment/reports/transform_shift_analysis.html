Which transform shifts the fewest tokens when a smooth motion is edited?

Generated 2026-11-17 20:51 by scripts/transform_shift_analysis.py. Synthetic shapes only; no dataset content.

Result. With every transform held to the same accuracy, a visible edit of a smooth shape changes on average 22% of the tokens for the best transform (orthogonal polynomials) and 89% for the worst (raw samples). DCT-II ranks 3 and Haar 17 of 18: DCT shifts 30% of its tokens, Haar 69%. Smooth global edits (slopes, curvature, amplitude changes) are compact in the smooth global bases (polynomials, DCT, learned PCA) and spread over every level of a wavelet. The local bump is the only edit where the polynomial basis falls to the bottom; there raw samples do best and the wavelets and DCT are close (Haar 42%, DCT 44%).

1. Method

Shapes. 400 random smooth shapes per signal length: a cosine (frequency 0.3–2.5 cycles per window), a cubic polynomial, a Gaussian bump (width 0.08–0.25) and a tanh step (width 0.08–0.3), each part present with probability 0.7, then scaled to peak-to-peak 2. No sharp edges. Sampled at N points, the length a chunk tokenizer sees.

Edits (sizes about 15–30% of the range, clearly visible):

slope up / downglobal, smoothx(t) ± 0.6 (t − 0.5)
offsetglobal, smoothx(t) + 0.3
growing / decaying amplitudeglobal, evolvingx(t)(1 + 0.6t) / x(t)(1 − 0.5t)

local bumplocalx(t) + 0.4 exp(−(t − μ)² / 2·0.06²), μ uniform in [0.2, 0.8]
time shiftglobal, evolvingx(t − 0.06): the motion starts 6% later
frequency driftglobal, evolvingx(t^1.25): the motion speeds up through the window

Transforms (18), each as an N × N matrix: raw samples (no transform); DCT-II (the transform inside FAST), DST-II, a real DFT; Walsh–Hadamard (sequency order); orthogonal polynomials (Legendre, orthonormalized on the sample grid); PCA learned from 4,000 other random shapes of the same family (the best possible linear basis for these shapes); and 11 wavelets in periodization mode, decomposed until the coarsest band holds 2 coefficients: haar, db2, db4, db6, sym4, sym6, coif1, coif2, bior2.2, bior4.4, dmey. The biorthogonal ones (bior2.2, bior4.4) are not orthonormal; the step rule below keeps them comparable.

Tokens and the metric. Every coefficient ci is quantized with step s / ‖S:,i‖, where S is the inverse transform; each token's rounding then adds the same reconstruction error, so equal s means equal accuracy for every transform (orthonormal transforms simply use step s). Main setting s = 0.02 on a range of 2 (about 1%), also checked at 0.01, 0.05. A token is shifted when its quantized bin differs between the original and the edited shape. Lower is better if we want a behaviour change to touch few tokens. N90 counts the fewest coefficients that carry 90% of the change, measured as reconstruction energy.

2. Example

The slope edit is a single degree-1 polynomial term, a few low DCT terms, but a non-zero detail coefficient at every position of every Haar level, because a ramp has a constant non-zero difference everywhere. The local bump is the opposite case: Haar's changes stay near the bump, while the polynomial basis needs many terms to build a narrow bump. Edits that change the whole motion over time (time shift, frequency drift, amplitude) move a large share of the tokens in every basis.

Longer windows lower the shifted fraction for every transform except raw samples, but the order barely changes: orthogonal polynomials, learned PCA and DCT lead at every length, the smooth wavelets (db4, sym, coif, bior4.4) come next, and Haar stays second to last, ahead only of raw samples.

5. What this does and does not say

  • It measures how many tokens a visible, smooth change of the motion touches. Fewer shifted tokens means a behaviour variant differs from the original in fewer places of the token sequence, which plausibly makes related motions easier to learn and share across tasks; this is a hypothesis, not tested here.
  • It agrees with the full-LIBERO result where DCT without BPE learned much faster than Haar DWT fixed (77% vs 58.5% after one epoch, both plain cross-entropy), and with the single-task result going the other way: on one task the policy only has to reproduce one motion, where variation across motions matters little.
  • The shapes are smooth by construction. Real action chunks also contain contact events and gripper switches, which favour local bases; the wavelet-selection report measures compactness on real LIBERO chunks instead.
  • Our deployed Haar codec splits a 10-step chunk into 8 + 2 segments and drops fine levels adaptively; this study uses whole-window transforms with every coefficient kept, so it ranks bases, not the deployed codecs.
  • PCA is learned from the same shape family (not from the edits), so it is a data-adapted reference rather than a fair competitor; it compresses the shapes best but is not the best for the edits.
  • Boundary handling matters. DCT-II implicitly mirrors the window, so a slope stays smooth at the edges; the real DFT and the longer periodized wavelets treat the window as periodic, so a slope becomes a jump at the edge (the DFT's poor slope scores). Haar's 2-tap filters never cross the window edge, so its slope cost comes from the ramp itself, not the boundary.
