Result. With subtle edits (a quarter of the visible size) and small smooth jitter on top, the edit is recovered from the token sequence 58% of the time with DCT-II (rank 11 of 18) and 68% with Haar (rank 3); chance is 10%. Best: Walsh–Hadamard (77%). Different edits of the same shape move overlapping token sets 35% of the time with DCT and 51% with Haar, DCT's largest token jump per edit is 47 bins against 38 for Haar, and its 3 most-changed tokens carry 80% of the change against 57%. So DCT writes each edit as a few strong, edit-specific tokens, while Haar spreads it over many tokens; the spreading is why token-mismatch matching recovers subtle edits more often with Haar.

1. Why count tokens differently from distances

Most transforms here are orthonormal, so they preserve distances: before quantization a trajectory change has exactly the same size in DCT, Haar or any orthonormal basis, and every one of them separates trajectories equally well. What differs is how the change is split over tokens. A token model predicts each token as a symbol from a vocabulary, so a change written as a few large jumps in specific tokens is a clear, edit-specific fingerprint, while the same change spread as ±1 nudges over most tokens looks like quantization noise and resembles every other edit. The metrics below measure that split on the quantized tokens.

2. Method

400 random smooth shapes, the 9 edits and the 18 transforms of the token-shift analysis, N = 32, equal-accuracy quantizer step 0.02 on a range of 2. Every metric is computed at the visible edit size and at a quarter of it (subtle edits: a slope of ±0.15 over the window, a 1.5% delay).

  • Peak jump: the largest |bin change| of any token, averaged over shapes and edits.
  • Top-3 share: share of the total |bin change| carried by the 3 most-changed tokens.
  • Overlap: for two different edits of the same shape, the Jaccard overlap of the token positions each one moves (lower = edits touch different tokens).
  • Identified: the original and its 9 edits give 10 clean token sequences. Each is perturbed by nuisance noise (4 draws) and matched back to the nearest clean sequence, either by the number of mismatched tokens (Hamming, the symbol view of a categorical decoder) or by summed |bin difference|. Accuracy; chance is 10%. Noise models: smooth jitter: a random smooth shape of the same family scaled to peak-to-peak 0.06 (3% of the range) plus white noise of standard deviation 0.003; white jitter: independent noise on every sample, standard deviation 0.01 (0.5% of the range).

5. Reading it

  • Two different notions of a distinctive fingerprint pull apart. DCT (with orthogonal polynomials and Walsh–Hadamard) writes each edit as a few large jumps in its own tokens: the highest peak jump, the highest top-3 share and among the lowest overlap between edits. Haar spreads every edit over many tokens with large overlap between edits.
  • But counting mismatched tokens rewards spreading: an edit that moves 70% of the tokens gives a nearest-match search many independent votes, so Haar recovers subtle edits under jitter more often than DCT. Concentration alone does not make edits easier to recognize by symbol matching.
  • Matched by summed |bin difference| every transform recovers the edit 89–100% of the time: the information is equally present in all of them (distances are preserved), and the differences come only from the symbol view.
  • So the case for DCT is about what a model has to learn, not raw separability: a family of similar motions shares most DCT tokens and differs in a few predictable ones, while in Haar the same small change rewrites most of the sequence. This study cannot tell which helps a policy more; the full-LIBERO results (DCT ahead of Haar) suggest the former.
  • The shapes are smooth by construction; real chunks with contacts and gripper switches are less favourable to global bases.
  • Identification uses 10 candidates per shape (the original and its 9 edits), all from the same shape; it measures separating variants of one motion, not separating unrelated motions.
