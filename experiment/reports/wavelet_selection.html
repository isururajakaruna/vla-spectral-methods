1. What the test does

  1. Cut demonstration actions into windows of H = 10, 32 steps; normalize with training-split mean/std. Calibration: 116525 (H=10), 99750 (H=32) training windows (stride 2); evaluation: 2674 (H=10), 783 (H=32) disjoint validation windows, all 40 tasks.
  2. Forward multilevel DWT per action dimension (6 continuous dims; the gripper is coded exactly by every variant and left out). Levels: 3 at H=10, 4 at H=32, giving coefficient groups cA (trend) and cD per level (detail, coarse → fine).
  3. Quantize every coefficient to 256 bins with a per-(level, dimension) step calibrated on training windows (same rule as the deployed codec).
  4. Keep the coarsest k groups and drop the finer detail. Fixed: the same k for every chunk. Adaptive: k chosen per chunk to minimize MSE + γ·tokens (one extra token states k), γ swept.
  5. Inverse DWT and compare with the original: mean squared error, worst-case error, error at the chunk's first/last step (where chunks join) and added roughness (jerk ratio).

Ranking metric: tokens per chunk needed to match FAST's reconstruction accuracy (FAST-libero validation MSE 6.51e-04 at H=10, 6.98e-04 at H=32), read off the adaptive rate–distortion curve (lower convex hull, linear interpolation = mixing two settings across chunks), divided by Haar's. Each transform uses its better boundary mode per horizon; the overall ranking averages the two horizons. Worst-case error, chunk-edge error and jerk at the FAST-matched operating point are reported alongside but not ranked: a transform can buy low worst-case error simply by spending more tokens.

Mixed transforms use a different wavelet at each decomposition level (finest level first). haar→… variants keep Haar's sharp steps at the finest level for abrupt events (contact, sudden stops) and use a smooth wavelet for the slow coarse motion; sym4→haar is the reverse ordering.

7. Caveats

  • This measures how well a transform can represent the motion, not how well a model can learn to predict its tokens. The winner still has to be trained and evaluated in simulation.
  • Depth is chosen per whole chunk; the deployed codec also searches over segmentations, which helps Haar most because Haar segments do not overlap.
  • Symmetric mode adds coefficients at the edges (redundant expansion); those extra coefficients are counted as tokens.
  • Biorthogonal transforms are not energy-preserving, so the same quantizer step gives different signal-domain noise; that effect is included in the measurements.
  • Ties and near-ties within a few percent are within the variation you would see from a different validation split.
